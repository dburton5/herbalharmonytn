Chamomile is the apple-scented, golden tea a lot of people reach for when the day is over and the house finally gets quiet. It has a reputation as a gentle evening cup: calming, easy on the stomach, and simple to brew. That reputation is old. The research behind it is more mixed, and more specific, than the bedtime stories usually let on.

This article is about German chamomile, the flower most often used in tea, and what we can honestly say about it.

## What is chamomile?

There are two plants people call chamomile. **German chamomile** (*Matricaria chamomilla*, also written *Matricaria recutita* or *Chamomilla recutita*) is the one in most tea bags and loose-leaf jars. **Roman chamomile** (*Chamaemelum nobile*) is a relative with its own traditional uses. The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) focuses its fact sheet on the German plant, and so does this article.

German chamomile is a small, daisy-like flower in the same plant family as ragweed, chrysanthemums, marigolds, and daisies. NCCIH notes that it appears in ancient medical writings and was an important herb in China, Egypt, Greece, and Rome. Today people still brew it for indigestion, a restless mind, sleep, and occasional skin or mouth irritation.

The flowers contain several plant compounds that researchers have studied in the lab, including the flavonoid **apigenin** and aromatic oils such as **bisabolol** and **chamazulene**. Those names show up often in explanations of why chamomile feels calming, but a lab finding is not the same thing as proof that a cup of tea will do a particular job in a person.

## What the research really says

NCCIH's plain summary is the right starting point: studies of chamomile for specific conditions have not produced enough reliable evidence to rate how useful it is in the clinic. Some trials also mix chamomile with other herbs, which makes it hard to know what chamomile itself is doing.

Here is what the stronger human studies actually tested.

**A modest effect on mild-to-moderate anxiety, in extract form.** In 2009, researchers at the University of Pennsylvania ran an eight-week randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial of a German chamomile extract in 57 adults with mild-to-moderate generalized anxiety disorder (GAD). The extract was made as 220 mg capsules standardized to 1.2% apigenin, and the daily amount could be increased up to five capsules (1,100 mg). People taking chamomile had a modestly greater drop in Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale scores than people taking placebo (P=0.047). Side-effect rates were similar. The authors called this the first controlled trial of chamomile extract for GAD and asked for larger studies to confirm it. ([Amsterdam et al., *Journal of Clinical Psychopharmacology*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3600416/))

**A later, longer look, still not a slam dunk.** A follow-up trial enrolled 179 people with moderate-to-severe GAD. Everyone first took 1,500 mg a day of a pharmaceutical-grade chamomile extract (500 mg, three times daily) for 12 weeks. Responders were then randomly assigned to stay on chamomile or switch to placebo for 26 weeks. Relapse was less common with chamomile (15.2% versus 25.5%), but that difference was not statistically significant. Anxiety scores did stay lower in the chamomile group, and side effects remained low. The researchers said a larger study would be needed to settle the relapse question. ([Mao et al., *Phytomedicine*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27912875/))

Those two trials matter, and they also have a limit that is easy to miss: they used **standardized extract capsules**, not a supermarket tea bag. A cup of tea is a different preparation, at a different dose, and it has not been tested in the same way.

**Sleep is more tradition than proof.** A 2019 systematic review and meta-analysis looked at chamomile for anxiety, GAD, insomnia, and sleep quality. It found a modest improvement in GAD scores at two and four weeks, and a signal for better sleep quality. It also found only one randomized trial on insomnia itself, and that trial did not show a significant change in insomnia severity. NCCIH cites this review when it says there is very little information on chamomile for insomnia, and that the one insomnia study found no benefit. ([Hieu et al., *Phytotherapy Research*](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31006899/); [Zick et al., 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21939549/))

**Digestion is mostly traditional use.** People have long reached for chamomile after a heavy meal or for gas, bloating, and stomach cramps. NCCIH notes that some combination products that include chamomile have been studied for diarrhea in children and for colic in infants, but chamomile alone has not been shown to help those conditions. So a cup for a unsettled stomach is a time-tested habit, not a proven treatment.

**Skin and mouth rinses are still preliminary.** Small studies have looked at chamomile mouthwashes after cancer treatment, and at topical use for the skin. Memorial Sloan Kettering's About Herbs monograph treats those findings as early, not settled.

> **The honest pattern:** Chamomile tea is a gentle, caffeine-free ritual with a long history. The best clinical evidence so far is a modest anxiety benefit from extract capsules in relatively small trials. Evidence that tea itself treats insomnia or an anxiety disorder is limited. A cup can still be a comforting part of an evening routine. It is not a substitute for care if worry or sleeplessness is getting in the way of daily life.

## How to brew a cup

For a simple tea, a common home method is:

1. Use **1 to 2 teaspoons of dried flowers** per cup of just-off-boiling water.
2. Pour the water over the flowers and **cover the cup** so the aroma stays in.
3. Steep **5 to 10 minutes**, then strain.
4. Drink **1 to 4 cups a day** if it agrees with you, often with the last cup in the evening.

Covering the cup matters more than it sounds. Chamomile's scent is part of the experience, and a lid keeps those light aromatic oils from drifting off with the steam.

Cooled tea is sometimes used as a compress on the skin. Keep it away from the eyes (NCCIH notes chamomile can irritate them), and skip topical use altogether if you have a daisy-family allergy.

## Who should be careful

Chamomile tea in ordinary food-like amounts is considered likely safe for most people, and side effects are uncommon. That still leaves some important exceptions.

- **Daisy-family allergies.** If you react to ragweed, chrysanthemums, marigolds, or daisies, skip chamomile. Allergic reactions can include a rash, asthma symptoms, and, rarely, a severe reaction. Memorial Sloan Kettering advises against chamomile if you are allergic to ragweed or flowers in the sunflower family.
- **Blood thinners.** Interactions with warfarin have been reported. If you take warfarin or another blood thinner, talk with your clinician or pharmacist before making chamomile a daily habit. It may raise the risk of bruising or bleeding.
- **Sedatives and other medicines.** There are theoretical reasons to think chamomile could add to the effects of sedatives, and NCCIH also notes reported interactions with some drugs that the liver metabolizes. Preliminary research has raised questions about chamomile and birth-control pills, and about conditions that are sensitive to estrogen. These are reasons to check with a clinician, not reasons to panic over a occasional cup.
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Little is known about safety in pregnancy or while nursing. Everyday tea amounts are commonly used, but medicinal-strength doses are better avoided unless a qualified clinician says otherwise.
- **Before a procedure.** If you have surgery or another procedure planned, tell your care team about any chamomile tea or supplements you use.

Chamomile is a supportive habit, not a treatment for an anxiety disorder, chronic insomnia, or a digestive disease. If symptoms are persistent, worsening, or getting in the way of work, school, or sleep, that is a conversation for a healthcare professional.

## The bottom line

A cup of German chamomile is a simple, caffeine-free way to close out the day. The plant has a long traditional use for calm and a settled stomach, and extract studies suggest a modest benefit for mild-to-moderate anxiety. The evidence that tea itself fixes insomnia is thin, and that honesty is useful. Enjoy the cup for what it is: a gentle ritual, not a prescription.
